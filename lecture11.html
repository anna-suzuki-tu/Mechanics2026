Learning Goals for This Lecture
  1. Identify the limitations of Newton's method that motivate analytical mechanics Newton's method requires identifying and resolving all constraint forces (normal forces, tensions) as explicit vectors. For systems with many degrees of freedom or complex constraints, this becomes unmanageable. Analytical mechanics replaces force vectors with scalar energy quantities.
  2. Define generalized coordinates and the Lagrangian L = T − V A generalized coordinate q is any independent variable that fully describes the system configuration. The Lagrangian L = T − V is the difference between kinetic and potential energy. Its form is the same regardless of which coordinates you choose.
  3. Experience the computational advantage on a simple system For a spring-mass system: write T = ½mẋ², V = ½kx², L = T − V. The equation of motion falls out in three lines — without drawing a single force diagram. Compare directly with Newton's method on the same problem.
Caution ⚠

The Lagrangian L = T − V is NOT the total energy (which is T + V). The sign difference is essential — using T + V gives the wrong equations of motion. A generalized coordinate must be independent; if constraints link your chosen variables, you have too many coordinates.

🟢 Basic Problems — Explained in Main Video
Basic 1
Degrees of Freedom and Generalized Coordinates
For each system below, state the number of degrees of freedom n and the most natural generalized coordinate(s): (1) a single particle sliding freely on a flat horizontal plane, (2) a simple pendulum (mass on rigid rod of length L, pinned at a fixed pivot, moving in a vertical plane), (3) a bead threaded on a straight frictionless wire, (4) a double pendulum (two masses on two rigid rods, pinned at a fixed pivot, moving in a vertical plane).
Basic 2
Virtual Work Equilibrium: Spring on Inclined Plane
A block of mass m rests on a smooth inclined plane of angle β, connected to a fixed wall by a spring of stiffness k (natural length at x = 0 along slope). Find the equilibrium position x_eq using the principle of virtual work. Note: the normal force does no virtual work — it never appears.
Basic 3
Virtual Work Equilibrium: Two Masses in a Semicylindrical Trough
A smooth semicylindrical trough of radius R holds a light rigid rod of length √2R. Masses 2m and m are attached at each end, resting on the curved surface. Find the equilibrium angle θ (angle of rod with horizontal) using the principle of virtual work. Hint: express heights y₁(θ) and y₂(θ) from the trough geometry, then set the total virtual work to zero.
🟡 Core Problems
Core 1
D'Alembert's Principle: Block on a Slope
A block of mass m slides without friction down an inclined plane of angle β. Using d'Alembert's principle with generalized coordinate x (displacement along slope), derive the equation of motion and find the acceleration. Compare with the Newtonian approach — how many equations does each method require?
Core 2
Virtual Work Equilibrium: Two-Mass Pulley System
Two masses m₁ and m₂ (m₁ > m₂) are connected by a light inextensible string over a frictionless massless pulley. Using the principle of virtual work with a single generalized coordinate, find the condition for equilibrium. Then apply d'Alembert's principle to derive the Atwood machine acceleration a = (m₁ − m₂)g / (m₁ + m₂). Note: the string tension never appears.
Core 3
Generalized Force: Pendulum with Applied Torque
A simple pendulum (mass m, length L, angle θ from vertical) has an external torque τ applied at the pivot (positive in direction of increasing θ). Find the generalized force Q_θ and write the equation of motion. Hint: Q_i = Σ F_applied · (∂r/∂q_i); the virtual work of torque τ through virtual rotation δθ is τ·δθ.
🔴 Advanced Problem — Optional
Advanced 1
Two-DOF System: Mass on a Rotating Arm
A light rigid arm of length L is pinned at the origin and rotates freely in a horizontal plane. At its tip, mass m can slide along the arm (frictionless). Let θ = arm angle, r = distance of mass from pivot. (1) State the DOF and generalized coordinates. (2) Write the mass position in Cartesian coordinates as a function of (r, θ). (3) Compute generalized forces Q_r and Q_θ (gravity does no work; no other applied forces). (4) What does Q_r = 0 and Q_θ = 0 tell you physically about conservation laws?
