Lecture 11 — Analytical Mechanics I: Why Lagrangian?

You identified Newton's limitations for constrained systems. You defined generalized coordinates and the Lagrangian L = T − V. You experienced how constraint forces disappear automatically when using scalar energy quantities, deriving the spring-mass equation of motion in three lines without a force diagram.

Learning Goals for This Lecture
  1. Derive and apply the Euler-Lagrange equation The Euler-Lagrange equation: d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = 0. The procedure is always the same: compute ∂L/∂q̇, differentiate with respect to time, then subtract ∂L/∂q. Apply it to each generalized coordinate independently.
  2. Recognize cyclic coordinates and their conservation law A coordinate q is cyclic if L does not depend on q — only on q̇. In that case, ∂L/∂q̇ = constant, meaning the corresponding generalized momentum is conserved. This is a direct route to conservation laws.
  3. Apply the Lagrangian method to multi-DOF systems and rigid bodies For a system with n degrees of freedom, write n Lagrange equations — one per generalized coordinate. Constraint forces that do no work never appear. This is why the Lagrangian scales to complex systems where Newton's method becomes intractable.
Caution ⚠

The Euler-Lagrange equation requires differentiating ∂L/∂q̇ with respect to TIME — not with respect to q. This is d/dt(∂L/∂q̇), not ∂²L/∂q̇². For non-conservative forces (friction, external loads), add the generalized force Q_i to the right side: d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = Q_i.
